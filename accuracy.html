How accurate is this site?

Every roll call page on this site comes from the House or Senate Clerk’s own roll call sheet, or, where there is none, from the legislature’s data feed or the chamber’s Journal. We check our pages against those records one General Court at a time, and we say here what each check found, mistakes included.

What has been read

  • Every General Court, 2009 to 2026: a check of the whole site. On 31 October 2026 we drew 1,800 roll calls at random, 200 from each General Court, and an AI model read each page claim by claim against the Clerk’s sheet: every name, mark, count, date and bill, what the vote was on, and anything else the page says. A second model checked every mistake it reported, and we read the hard cases ourselves against the sheet and the Journal. On the pages published before this check, 396 of the 1,798 roll calls with a sheet (22 percent) had at least one mistake. Most were the kinds the readings above had already fixed. The check found five more, now fixed on every page: 90 House pages showed an amendment from another bill; 1,567 Senate pages said “0 absent or not voting” where the sheet prints no such count; 493 House pages of 2009 to 2017 left off the legislative sitting the sheet prints beside its date; 332 House pages of 2018 and 2019 left off the question the sheet prints in full under the names; and three reissued 2015 sheets did not say they are marked Corrected. Then the 1,230 of them whose page had changed were read again on the rebuilt pages, and the other 568 had not changed: none of the 1,798 was wrong or missing. With a sample this size, the share of pages still wrong is very likely under a quarter of one percent.

  • House, 194th General Court (2025 to 2026): every roll call read. On 27 October 2026 AI models read all 235 House roll calls of the 194th from the Clerk's own sheets, without seeing our pages, and a script compared every name, mark, tally, date, bill and question with the page. One page was wrong: No. 154, a quorum call on 8 April 2026, lost the sheet's caption and its date. It was fixed. Then 50 roll calls drawn at random were read again by new readers: none was wrong. Six errors planted in a copy of the data, and two planted in the pages, were all caught by the checks that now run on every build.

  • Senate, 194th General Court (2025 to 2026): every roll call read. On 27 October 2026 AI models read all 238 Senate roll calls of the 194th from the Clerk's own sheets (and the first, the election of the President, from the Journal of the Senate), without seeing our pages, and a script compared every name, group, count, bill and question with the page. Scripts also checked every name on every sheet against the legislature's data feed. 109 pages were wrong in one or more of four ways: 107 showed a line from the bill's history as the question instead of the question the sheet prints; 14 showed an amendment the vote was not on; 5 showed no bill; and on 2, Nos. 17 and 69, the sheet and the bill's history name different bills, which the pages now say. All were fixed. Three roll calls (Nos. 110, 144 and 183) have no date in any record we hold, and their pages say so. Then 50 roll calls drawn at random were read again by new readers: none was wrong. Eight errors planted in a copy of the pages were all caught by the checks that now run on every build.

  • House, 193rd General Court (2023 to 2024): every roll call read. On 27 October 2026 AI models read all 203 House roll calls of the 193rd from the Clerk's own sheets, without seeing our pages, and a script compared every name, mark, tally, date, bill and question with the page. Scripts also read every sheet a second time from its text, including the 32 sheets of early 2023 printed in an older layout. No page was wrong. Then 50 roll calls drawn at random were read again by new readers: none was wrong. Errors planted in copies of the data and the pages were all caught by the checks that now run on every build.

  • Senate, 193rd General Court (2023 to 2024): every roll call read. On 27 October 2026 AI models read the Senate roll calls of the 193rd from the Clerk's own sheets (the election of the President, and No. 241, from the Journal of the Senate), without seeing our pages, and a script compared every name, group, count, bill and question with the page. One page was wrong: No. 241, a vote at about 2:30 in the morning to let the Senate sit past midnight, has no Clerk's sheet on the legislature's server, and its page left out a senator the Journal records as answering present. It now follows the Journal and cites its page. Two roll calls, Nos. 157 and 175 of May 2024, have no sheet and no published Journal; their pages show the legislature's data feed and say so. Then 50 roll calls drawn at random were read again by new readers: none was wrong.

  • House and Senate, 192nd General Court (2021 to 2022): every roll call read. On 27 October 2026 AI models read 539 of the 541 roll calls of the 192nd from the Clerk's own sheets (two Senate roll calls from the Journal of the Senate), without seeing our pages, and a script compared every name, mark, count, date, bill and question with the page. The two Senate roll calls the legislature keeps no sheet for, Nos. 106 and 210, were checked against its data feed. Three House pages were wrong: No. 1 of 2021, a quorum call, lost its caption; No. 38 of 2021 showed only the tail of its headline; and a sheet the Clerk's 2022 volume numbers 0 and marks unofficial was shown and counted as a roll call, though the House Journal records no such vote. The first two were fixed. The third now has a page that says what the records show, and it is counted nowhere. Then 100 roll calls drawn at random, 50 from each chamber, were read again by new readers: none was wrong. Eighteen errors planted in a copy of the data and the pages were all caught by the checks that now run on every build.

  • House and Senate, 191st General Court (2019 to 2020): every roll call read. On 30 October 2026 AI models read 920 of the 923 roll calls of the 191st from the Clerk's own sheets, without seeing our pages, and a script compared every name, mark, count, date, bill and question with the page. The three Senate roll calls the legislature keeps no sheet for, Nos. 302, 308 and 309, were checked against its data feed. 40 pages were wrong in one way: the page said only "Roll call" where the sheet prints what the vote was on. On 38 House pages that was a quorum call, a motion to suspend a House rule, or a bill printed without its period ("H 4820"); on 2 Senate pages, Nos. 14 and 66, a question with no bill. All were fixed. Then 100 roll calls drawn at random, 50 from each chamber, were read again by new readers: none was wrong. Eighteen errors planted in a copy of the data and the pages were all caught by the checks that now run on every build.

  • House and Senate, 188th and 189th General Courts (2013 to 2016): every roll call read. On 31 October 2026 AI models read 2,219 of the 2,221 roll calls of these two General Courts from the Clerk's own sheets, without seeing our pages. The House sheets of these years are scanned pages, and the models read every name and mark off the picture. A script compared every name, mark, count, date, bill and question with the page. The two Senate roll calls the legislature keeps no sheet for were checked against its data feed. 562 pages were wrong. On 551 House pages the headline dropped or garbled part of the question the sheet prints: a whole line holding the word "Representatives", a word split in two ("not withstanding"), or a hyphen lost at a line's end. On 11 Senate pages: three showed an amendment other than the one the sheet names, and now say that the sheet and the bill's history disagree; two showed an amendment's later fate beside a losing vote; one headline was cut short; one showed no question; and on four, the Clerk's sheet lists a senator twice, as voting and as absent, and the Journal of the Senate records only the vote; those pages now list the vote once and say so. All were fixed. Then 200 roll calls drawn at random, 50 from each chamber in each General Court, were read again by new readers: none was wrong. Twenty-seven errors planted in a copy of the data and the pages were all caught by the checks that now run on every build.

  • House, 186th and 187th General Courts (2009 to 2012): every roll call read. On 31 October 2026 AI models read all 887 House roll calls of 2009 to 2012 that have a page from the printed sheets (the State Library's bound volume for 2009 and 2010, the Clerk's for 2011 and 2012), without seeing our pages, and a script compared every name, mark, count, date and question with the page. The 888th, 2012 No. 214, was expunged by order of the House and has no tally. 254 pages were wrong in one way: the question. On 238 the scanned sheet's hidden text had been read badly (a line dropped, a word broken as "transpor - tation", a stray date at the front). On 16 more the hidden text had spaces inside whole words ("environ men tal"), or lost a hyphen at a line's end. All were fixed. Then 100 roll calls drawn at random, 50 from each court, were read again by new readers: none was wrong. Eleven errors planted in a copy of the data and the pages were all caught by the checks that now run on every build. The Senate's roll calls of 2009 to 2012 are not here to read: the legislature keeps no sheet for them, and its data feed holds only 28 of the 186th's.

What has not been read yet

The 190th General Court (2017 to 2018), House and Senate, has not been read in full yet. The whole-site check above read 200 of its roll calls; the rest carry only the checks a script can make. No person has read the record yet: every reading above was done by AI models.

Missing: the Senate’s roll calls of 2009 to 2012

What is missing. The Senate numbered at least 410 roll calls in 2009 and 2010, and at least 316 in 2011 and 2012. This site has 28 of the first, and none of the second. Apart from these, every roll call number the House and Senate list from 2009 on has a page here, or a written reason it has none.

Why. The legislature publishes Senate roll calls in two places a site like this can read: a data feed, and the Senate Clerk’s own roll call sheets. For these four years the feed holds only the 28 above, and the legislature’s server had no Clerk’s sheet for any of them we asked for. The votes are not lost. They are printed in the Journal of the Senate, the Senate’s own daily record, which we have not yet read for these years.

What you can do. If you need how a senator voted between 2009 and 2012, open the Journal of the Senate on the legislature’s site, choose the month, and find the day’s yeas and nays. A senator’s page here counts only the votes this site holds, so for those four years it is not their whole record. A vote missing here is not an absence.
