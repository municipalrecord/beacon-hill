How accurate is this site?

Every roll call page on this site comes from the House or Senate Clerk’s own roll call sheet, or, where there is none, from the legislature’s data feed or the chamber’s Journal. We check our pages against those records one General Court at a time, and we say here what each check found, mistakes included.

What has been read

  • Every General Court, 2009 to 2026: a check of the whole site. On 31 October 2026 we drew 1,800 roll calls at random, 200 from each General Court, and an AI model read each page claim by claim against the Clerk’s sheet: every name, mark, count, date and bill, what the vote was on, and anything else the page says. A second model checked every mistake it reported, and we read the hard cases ourselves against the sheet and the Journal. On the pages published before this check, 396 of the 1,798 roll calls with a sheet (22 percent) had at least one mistake. Most were the kinds the readings above had already fixed. The check found five more, now fixed on every page: 90 House pages showed an amendment from another bill; 1,567 Senate pages said “0 absent or not voting” where the sheet prints no such count; 493 House pages of 2009 to 2017 left off the legislative sitting the sheet prints beside its date; 332 House pages of 2018 and 2019 left off the question the sheet prints in full under the names; and three reissued 2015 sheets did not say they are marked Corrected. Then the 1,230 of them whose page had changed were read again on the rebuilt pages, and the other 568 had not changed: none of the 1,798 was wrong or missing. With a sample this size, the share of pages still wrong is very likely under a quarter of one percent.

  • House, 194th General Court (2025 to 2026): every roll call read. On 27 October 2026 AI models read all 235 House roll calls of the 194th from the Clerk's own sheets, without seeing our pages, and a script compared every name, mark, tally, date, bill and question with the page. One page was wrong: No. 154, a quorum call on 8 April 2026, lost the sheet's caption and its date. It was fixed. Then 50 roll calls drawn at random were read again by new readers: none was wrong. Six errors planted in a copy of the data, and two planted in the pages, were all caught by the checks that now run on every build.

  • Senate, 194th General Court (2025 to 2026): every roll call read. On 27 October 2026 AI models read all 238 Senate roll calls of the 194th from the Clerk's own sheets (and the first, the election of the President, from the Journal of the Senate), without seeing our pages, and a script compared every name, group, count, bill and question with the page. Scripts also checked every name on every sheet against the legislature's data feed. 109 pages were wrong in one or more of four ways: 107 showed a line from the bill's history as the question instead of the question the sheet prints; 14 showed an amendment the vote was not on; 5 showed no bill; and on 2, Nos. 17 and 69, the sheet and the bill's history name different bills, which the pages now say. All were fixed. Three roll calls (Nos. 110, 144 and 183) have no date in any record we hold, and their pages say so. Then 50 roll calls drawn at random were read again by new readers: none was wrong. Eight errors planted in a copy of the pages were all caught by the checks that now run on every build.

  • House and Senate, 188th and 189th General Courts (2013 to 2016): every roll call read. On 31 October 2026 AI models read 2,219 of the 2,221 roll calls of these two General Courts from the Clerk's own sheets, without seeing our pages. The House sheets of these years are scanned pages, and the models read every name and mark off the picture. A script compared every name, mark, count, date, bill and question with the page. On 551 House pages the headline dropped or garbled part of the question the sheet prints: a whole line holding the word "Representatives", a word split in two ("not withstanding"), or a hyphen lost at a line's end. On 11 Senate pages: three showed an amendment other than the one the sheet names, and now say that the sheet and the bill's history disagree; two showed an amendment's later fate beside a losing vote; one headline was cut short; one showed no question; and on four, the Clerk's sheet lists a senator twice, as voting and as absent, and the Journal of the Senate records only the vote; those pages now list the vote once and say so. Twenty-seven errors planted in a copy of the data and the pages were all caught by the checks that now run on every build.

  • House, 186th and 187th General Courts (2009 to 2012): every roll call read. On 31 October 2026 AI models read all 887 House roll calls of 2009 to 2012 that have a page from the printed sheets (the State Library's bound volume for 2009 and 2010, the Clerk's for 2011 and 2012), without seeing our pages, and a script compared every name, mark, count, date and question with the page.   • House and Senate, 190th General Court (2017 to 2018): every roll call read. On 31 October 2026 AI models read all 1,084 roll calls of the 190th General Court that have a page, House and Senate, twice each: once from the Clerk’s sheet alone, without seeing our page, with a script comparing every name, mark, count, date, bill and question with the page; and once page against sheet, claim by claim. One House question had joined two words across a dash (“Collaborativecomputer”); one House page marked a vote as recorded after the roll closed where the sheet has no star; three Senate pages showed an amendment other than the one the sheet names; on two Senate pages the sheet’s own amendment number or mover differs from the Journal of the Senate, and those pages now quote the Journal; and four showed word-processor codes inside an amendment’s text. Twenty-seven errors planted in a copy of the data and the pages were all caught by the checks that now run on every build.

  • Senate, 186th and 187th General Courts (2009 to 2012): every roll call the Journal of the Senate prints, read against the Journal. The legislature keeps no Clerk's sheet for these years and its data feed holds 28 roll calls of 2010 and none of 2011 or 2012, so on 31 October 2026 the Senate's roll calls of 2009 to 2012 were read from the Journal of the Senate, the Senate's own daily record, as the legislature publishes it online. A parser was proved on twenty roll calls read by eye (20 of 20), then run over every sitting the Journal lists for the four years and two sittings the Internet Archive holds. It produced 452 pages: 153 of the 186th and 299 of the 187th, each citing the Journal's PDF and page. Then AI models read every one of those pages, and 21 pages of 2010 whose date the Journal supplied, claim by claim against the Journal's own text, without seeing an earlier reading. 41 pages were wrong on the first build: 18 headings said no question was published where the Journal's sentence names one; 11 pages said of a pair of senators what the Clerk's sheets say and the Journal does not; 7 closing sentences were cut at an initial; the two election pages left off the Chair's totals and one sentence; two times the Journal garbles were missing, and a few notes about the Journal's own counts were worded wrongly. 81 pages also gained the bill the Journal's sentence names. All were fixed, every changed page was read again blind, and on the final build none of the 473 pages was wrong and none left anything off; 142 differ from the Journal only in the form of a name (the site prints names as the legislature's member list does). The Journal prints a placeholder instead of names for 173 roll calls of 2009 to 2010 and two of 2011, and 71 numbers fall on days the online Journal does not publish; each is listed above with its reason. The reader that did this was first scored on the 48 faults banked from earlier Beacon Hill audits and caught 45.

The Senate’s roll calls of 2009 to 2012: read from the Journal

Where these pages come from. For these four years the legislature’s data feed holds 28 Senate roll calls (all of 2010) and its server has the Clerk’s sheet for 26 of them; it holds none of 2011 or 2012. The rest are read from the Journal of the Senate, the Senate’s own daily record, which prints each roll call’s question, time, tally and names. The method, and the twenty roll calls read by eye against the Journal’s page to prove it, are on the methods page.

What the Journal does not give. The Senate numbered 410 roll calls in 2009 and 2010. This site has 181 with every name. For 173 the online Journal prints a placeholder (“INSERT ROLL CALL”) where the names should be: Nos. 31, 76 to 78, 80, 83 to 90, 94 to 97, 124 to 137, 193 to 286, 306 to 347, 349 to 354. For 56 the day’s Journal is not online at all: Nos. 79, 81 to 82, 188 to 189, 355 to 387, 389 to 399, 401 to 407. In 2011 and 2012 the Senate numbered 316; this site has 299; 2 print no names (Nos. 25, 31) and 15 fall on days not online (Nos. 14 to 15, 56 to 62, 175, 260 to 264). For each of these the Journal’s own line, where it prints one, is kept as the reason the number has no page.

A caution. The Journal of the Senate online for 2009 to 2012 is the Clerk’s unproofed daily text, not the bound, proofread volume; the Senate’s own notice on each day’s PDF says so. Where the Journal’s own figures disagree with each other (a count printed after a list that its summary and its names both contradict), the page shows the names as printed and says what the Journal says. Nothing is corrected silently.

What you can do. If you need a roll call that has no page here, the Journal of the Senate on the legislature’s site is the place to ask for it, and the Senate Clerk holds the roll call sheets. A senator’s page here counts only the votes this site holds, so for 2009 and 2010 it is not their whole record. A vote missing here is not an absence.
