Why you cannot find out how your representative voted

Yeas and Nays

Most of what the Massachusetts House does leaves no record of how anybody voted. That is not an oversight. It is written down.

The House adopts a set of rules for itself at the start of every two year session, numbered 1 to 101. Almost nobody outside the building reads them. They are worth reading, because they decide what the public is allowed to find out, and they answer a question people ask constantly without knowing where to look.

44Rule 44
Informal sessions

There are two kinds of session, and only one of them can take a vote

The Speaker decides when the House meets informally. An informal session is not a lesser version of a formal one. It is a different machine, and a great deal of real legislation passes through it.

Formal debate, or the taking of the sense of the House by yeas and nays shall not be conducted during such informal session.

House Rule 44

In an informal session a roll call is not merely unusual. It is prohibited. Business moves by voice, and the record shows that the House acted without showing who wanted it to. If you go looking for your representative's position on something that passed informally, there is nothing to find, and no amount of persistence will produce it.

52Rule 52
Taking the yeas and nays

In a formal session, one member cannot get a roll call. Sixteen can.

The threshold is fixed by the House's own rule, and it is a share of everyone elected rather than everyone in the room.

The sense of the House shall be taken by yeas and nays whenever required by ten percent of the members elected.

House Rule 52

16 of 160. Below that line, the House votes by voice and the record keeps no names. The rule also gives the Speaker up to twelve minutes before putting the question, and then holds the machine open between two and twenty two minutes.

Reading the sheet

When a roll call does happen, this is what it looks like

The House prints one sheet per vote. Four columns of surnames, each with a single letter pressed against it. Below is the first recorded vote of the 2025 session, reproduced from the House's own published volume. It is the same vote that runs behind the title of this page.

YYeaA vote in favor.
NNayA vote against.
XNot votingOften an arranged absence. Under Rule 48 a member gives the Clerk written notice, and the Clerk then disables that member's voting station.
PPresentA deliberate act, not a shrug. The member must tell the Clerk in person after the vote is ordered and before it is announced.
*After voteRecorded once the roll had already closed.

That last mark is the one loose thread. The rule that allows a member to be recorded after the roll closes, within five minutes, sits inside the passage that applies when the electronic voting system has broken down. Asterisks appear on these sheets 965 times in nine years, which is far more often than the machines can plausibly have failed. Either the practice is broader than the text, or the marks come from somewhere the rules do not say. It is a fair question to put to the Clerk.

17BRule 17B
Committee votes

Committee votes are required to be public. Finding one is another matter.

Nearly every bill dies in committee, so the committee vote is often the only vote a bill ever gets. The rule on this is unambiguous.

The vote on any legislation in committee shall be a recorded vote of the full committee. … The record of all such roll calls shall be posted on the official website of the General Court within 48 hours of the vote.

House Rule 17B

They are posted. They are also close to invisible. A committee vote does not appear on the bill's page as it loads, and it is not in the legislature's public data feed. It lives behind a tab that only fills in if you click it. Technically published, practically hidden.

What you get when you find one is better than a floor vote, because it sorts every member of the committee into four groups rather than two.

FavorableMove it forward.
AdverseKill it.
Reserve RightDeclining to commit either way, while keeping the option to argue later.
No ActionTook no position at all.

The last two have no equivalent on the floor, where a member has only yea, nay, present or absent. Reserving your right is not an abstention and it is not an absence. It is its own thing, and any tally that folds it into either one is describing something that did not happen.

4Rules 4, 5 and 18
The chair

The Speaker's power is mostly the power to schedule

The rules give the Speaker the ordinary gavel authority you would expect, and one sentence of unusual plainness about voting.

In all cases the Speaker may vote.

House Rule 4

That is the whole rule. There is no custom of the chair sitting out, and in practice the Speaker votes on nearly every roll call. The real leverage sits elsewhere. Rule 44 lets the Speaker decide when the House meets informally, which decides when a roll call is possible at all. Rule 5 lets the Speaker hand the chair to any member. Rule 18 puts the appointment and removal of the majority leadership in the Speaker's hands. Rule 1A stops the House from convening before ten in the morning or sitting past midnight, unless every member present agrees to keep going.

33FRule 33F
Consolidated amendments

One guaranteed floor of record, added in 2017

During a budget the House bundles hundreds of individual amendments into a handful of consolidated ones and adopts them together. A rule added on February 2, 2017 makes that bundling impossible to do quietly.

No consolidated amendment shall be adopted except by a roll call vote.

House Rule 33F

It is one sentence, and it is the reason a reliable share of every year's recorded votes are budget consolidations. When you see a rule that short, it usually means somebody once did the opposite.

A body of 160 people, legislating for seven million, leaves roughly 150 recorded votes in a year, and on 43 percent of those nobody at all is on the losing side. That is not a chamber that fails to disagree. It is a chamber whose rules move disagreement to the places where the rules do not require a record.

ClockWhen the votes
are taken

The House votes in the afternoon. The Senate keeps going.

Every roll call the record dates to the hour: 1,370 in the House since 2018 and 1,821 in the Senate since 2013. The House's busiest hour is 14:00, the Senate's 16:00. The House takes almost nothing before 11 in the morning, which is Rule 1A's 10 AM floor and the informal-session habit together; the Senate has a morning. What both chambers vote on after midnight is mostly what the Constitution makes them record, borrowing and land takings, taken at the end of the last night because they cannot be skipped.
